04
Real-World Analysis

Case Study Analysis

Case Study: Data Privacy Scandal
Facebook and Cambridge Analytica

One of the biggest privacy scandals in recent history. About 87 million Facebook users had their personal information collected and used without their knowledge to influence elections around the world.

4.1 What Happened

The Story

In 2013, a researcher named Aleksandr Kogan made a simple personality quiz on Facebook. Around 270,000 people took the quiz and agreed to share their data. The problem was that Facebook's system at the time also gave the app access to all of those users' friends. Those friends never agreed to anything. In the end, data from about 87 million people was collected without anyone telling them.

Kogan then passed all of that data to a company called Cambridge Analytica. They used it to build detailed profiles of voters to figure out what kinds of messages each person would respond to. They then sold those profiles to political campaigns. Their biggest clients were the Trump presidential campaign in 2016 and the Brexit "Leave" campaign in the UK. The story became public in March 2018. Facebook's CEO Mark Zuckerberg was called to explain himself before the US Senate, and Facebook was fined 5 billion dollars by regulators.

4.2 The Ethical Problems

What Was Wrong About It

  • People never agreed to it. The 87 million people whose data was taken never said yes. Only a small group agreed to the quiz. The data of all their friends was swept up too. That is a serious violation of trust.
  • It was used to influence how people voted. Using someone's private information to push specific political messages at them without them knowing is a form of manipulation. People deserve to make up their own minds without being secretly pushed in a direction.
  • Facebook did not protect its users. Facebook had the ability to stop this kind of data sharing. They had rules in place but did not follow through on them. That is a failure of responsibility.
  • Personal beliefs were treated like a product. Things like your political opinions, religion, and relationships were turned into data that got bought and sold for profit. That is a violation of something deeply personal.
  • Facebook stayed quiet for years. They knew about this long before it became public, and they did not tell users. That silence made everything worse and broke people's trust even further.
4.3 Viewing It Through Ethical Theories

What Different Ethical Frameworks Say

Deontology (Duty-Based Ethics)

This approach says some actions are just wrong no matter the outcome. Using millions of people's data without asking them is wrong because it treats people as tools instead of as humans who deserve respect. A simple rule to follow: always ask for permission before using someone's personal information. If everyone followed that rule, this scandal would never have happened.

Virtue Ethics (Character-Based)

This approach focuses on who you are and not just what you do. An honest and caring company would have been upfront with users about how their data was being used. Facebook's attitude of moving fast and figuring things out later shows a culture that did not value honesty or fairness. That is a character problem, not just a policy problem.

Utilitarianism (What is Best for the Most)

This approach asks: did this help more people than it hurt? The answer is clearly no. Millions of people lost control of their private information. Public trust in social media dropped. Governments had to step in with stricter laws that affected the whole industry. Whatever political benefit came from it was far outweighed by the damage it caused.

4.4 How We Can Prevent This

What Should Be Done Differently

  • Make consent clear and simple. Apps and platforms should ask users in plain language what data they are collecting and exactly how it will be used. Not buried in fine print. And users should be able to change or take back that permission at any time.
  • Only collect what you actually need. If an app just needs your name for a quiz, it does not need your location, religion, and list of friends too. Collecting extra data just in case is how these problems start.
  • Check on the developers using your platform. Facebook gave outside developers access to huge amounts of user data and did not check what they were doing with it. Platforms need to actually follow up and make sure their rules are being followed.
  • Stronger laws with real consequences. The EU's GDPR law came partly from this scandal. It requires companies to tell users when their data is affected and gives people the right to ask for their data to be deleted. More countries need rules like this.
  • Teach ethics to developers. The people who built Facebook's data-sharing tools probably never thought about how they could be misused. Teaching IT students like us to think about the human impact of what we build is one of the most important things we can do.

My Commitment

Professional Ethics Pledge

This is my personal promise to myself and to the people who will one day be affected by the systems I build. It is not a list of rules someone gave me. It is what I actually believe.

I, as a future Information Technology professional, make the following pledge:
My Personal IT Ethics Pledge
  • I.

    I will protect people's private information like it matters, because it does.

    Behind every piece of data is a real person. I will treat any information people trust me with carefully and respectfully. I won't collect what I don't need, and I won't share what wasn't meant to be shared.

  • II.

    I will never use my skills to hurt or take advantage of people.

    Knowing how to build technology is a kind of power. I promise not to use that power to bully, harass, stalk, or manipulate anyone, online or off. No project is worth doing if it causes harm to a real person.

  • III.

    I will be honest when I find something that puts people at risk.

    If I ever find a flaw or weakness in a system, I will handle it the right way, by telling the right people so it can be fixed, rather than hiding it or using it for personal gain. People's safety comes first.

  • IV.

    I will be upfront about what I can and can't do.

    I won't oversell my work or promise things I can't deliver, especially when security is involved. If something isn't safe to launch, I'll say so, even if there's pressure not to.

  • V.

    I will keep learning, because technology keeps changing.

    New tools like AI and biometrics bring new questions that we don't have all the answers to yet. I want to stay curious and keep asking what the right thing to do is, not just when I graduate but throughout my whole career.

  • VI.

    I will speak up when something feels wrong, even when it's hard.

    Staying quiet when you see something bad happening is also a choice. If a project is heading in a harmful direction, I want to be someone who says something, respectfully but clearly. That's what I think a good IT professional looks like.
